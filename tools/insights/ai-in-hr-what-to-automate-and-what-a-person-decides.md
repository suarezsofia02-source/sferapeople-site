AI can take a lot of repetitive work off an HR team. It can also make bad decisions faster if it is used in the wrong place. This guide explains where AI agents and automations help in HR and recruiting, where a person has to stay in charge, and how to start without creating new problems.

## What we mean by AI agents and automation

- **Automation** follows fixed rules: when a candidate applies, add them to the tracker and send a confirmation.
- **AI assistants and agents** read, summarize, draft and suggest. An agent can also take several steps toward a goal, for example reading a profile, comparing it with a scorecard and preparing notes.

Both are tools. The question is never "can AI do this?" but "who is responsible for the result?"

## Where AI helps

These tasks are repetitive, low risk and easy to review:

- **Drafting.** First versions of job posts, outreach messages, interview guides and candidate emails, which a person edits before sending.
- **Summaries.** Interview notes, application forms and long threads, turned into a short and consistent format.
- **Organization.** Updating the tracker, scheduling, reminders and follow-ups so nothing is forgotten.
- **Reporting.** Time per stage, drop-off points and source quality, without building reports by hand every week.
- **Support for sourcing.** Helping research companies and profiles, with a person checking the results before anyone is contacted.

## Where a person must decide

Some decisions should not be delegated to a tool:

- Who moves forward and who is rejected.
- Which candidates are contacted and what is promised to them.
- The offer, the pay and the contract terms.
- Anything that needs judgment about a person, context or fairness.

AI can prepare information for these decisions. A person makes them and is accountable for them.

## The risks to check before you start

- **Bias.** Tools can repeat patterns in the data they learn from. Review results regularly and do not let a score replace a conversation.
- **Privacy.** Candidate data is personal data. Check what a tool does with the information you give it, where it is stored and what your privacy policy and local data protection rules require.
- **Transparency.** Candidates should not be surprised that a tool was used. Be clear about how their information is handled.
- **Rules about automated hiring tools.** Some places have specific rules for tools that screen or rank candidates, for example New York City and the European Union. They are different in each jurisdiction and they change, so ask a lawyer what applies to you.
- **Accuracy.** AI can sound sure and be wrong. Everything that reaches a candidate or a decision should be reviewed.

This is general information, not legal advice.

## How to start: one workflow at a time

1. **Pick one painful, repeatable task.** For example, writing candidate updates or keeping the tracker current.
2. **Write the steps down.** If you cannot describe the process, you cannot automate it well.
3. **Add a human check before anything is sent or decided.** Nothing should go to a candidate without a person approving it.
4. **Measure it.** Time saved, mistakes found and candidate feedback.
5. **Improve, then add the next workflow.** Small steps beat a big system nobody trusts.

## A simple HR setup with AI for a small company

If you have no People team yet, this is a reasonable base:

- One place that tracks candidates and stages.
- A written hiring process with a scorecard per role.
- Templates for the messages you send most often, drafted with AI and reviewed by a person.
- A few automations for confirmations, reminders and updates to the tracker.
- A short weekly view of what is open, what is stuck and who owns the next step.

You do not need a large system. You need a clear process first and then tools that support it. If you are still defining the process, start with [how to set up your hiring process](/insights/hiring-process-before-your-first-hires).
