Most teams hire first and build the process later. It works for the first hire or two, and then it breaks: interviews pile up, nobody knows who decides, candidates wait weeks for an answer and the best ones take another offer.

This guide is for two kinds of companies: startups without a People team, where the founder is also the recruiter, and companies that just raised a round and need to grow their teams fast without losing quality. The steps are the same. Only the size changes.

## 1. Write down the role before you open the search

Before you post anything, answer four questions in one page: what this person will own in the first six months, which skills are non-negotiable, which are nice to have, and what pay range and setup you can offer. If the team cannot agree on these, no process will fix the search.

## 2. Pick one owner per search

Every open role needs one person who owns it: who keeps the pipeline moving, who answers candidates and who calls the decision. In a small company that is usually the founder or the hiring manager. In a larger one it can be a recruiter. Without an owner, candidates wait and nobody notices.

## 3. Design the stages and keep them few

A simple process for most roles looks like this:

- A short intro call to check fit, pay and setup.
- One technical or skills conversation, with a clear scorecard.
- One conversation with the team or the manager.
- A decision and an offer.

Four stages is enough for most roles. In a small poll of 67 people in our network, 43% said that 5+ interview rounds make them drop out of a hiring process. Each extra round needs a reason. ## 4. Use a scorecard, not a feeling

Pick three to five things that matter for the role and score every candidate on the same ones, with a short note on the evidence. A scorecard makes interviews comparable, reduces bias from first impressions and makes the final decision faster, because you compare notes and not memories.

## 5. Set response times and keep them

Decide how fast you answer at each stage, for example a reply within a few working days after every interview, and make sure someone is responsible for it. Candidates read silence as a no, and senior people have other options. Only promise a timeline you can keep.

## 6. Track everything in one place

You need one place that shows each candidate, the stage they are in, who owns the next step and when it was last updated. It can be a spreadsheet at the start and a proper applicant tracking system later. What matters is that the information lives in one place and not in inboxes. This is also the base for measuring: how long each stage takes, where candidates drop out and which sources bring the best people.

## 7. Prepare the offer and the first 30 days

Know your range and your setup before the last interview. After the offer, a short written plan for the first month (goals, who the person reports to, who answers their questions) makes the difference between a hire who ramps up and one who gets lost.

## Common mistakes

- Opening the search before the team agrees on the role.
- Adding interview rounds to compensate for a lack of clear criteria.
- Leaving feedback to whoever has time.
- Sharing the pay range only at the end.
- Measuring nothing, so every search starts from zero.
