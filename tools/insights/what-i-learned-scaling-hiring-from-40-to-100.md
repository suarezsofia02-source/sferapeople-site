I was the Talent Acquisition Lead at a SaaS company in Mexico during the year it went from 40 people to 100. I was the first person there dedicated to hiring full time, I reported to the CEO and later to the CTPO, and I learned most of this the hard way.

If you are a founder somewhere around 40 people and hiring is starting to hurt, here is what I would tell you.

## Your bottleneck is probably the decision, not the pipeline

When I arrived, an external consultancy was doing the recruiting and two C-level executives had the final word on every hire. That works at 40 people. It does not work at 100, and it was not failing because nobody could find candidates.

Before you spend money on more sourcing, look at where candidates actually sit waiting. Most of the time it is a decision nobody owns.

## Build the process before you buy the tool

The first thing I built was not a system. It was a document that defined which interviews every process would have: the ones that repeat in every search, like the culture conversation, and the ones that change with the area and the role.

At 40 people I would not start with an ATS. Start with clear stages, a consistent set of interviews, and a shared idea of what each one is evaluating. That company never bought an ATS, and we still hired 60 people.

## When hiring managers resist, it is usually about time

The resistance I found was not with leadership. It was with hiring managers, and it showed up as pushback on moving candidates through the funnel and on logging anything in a system.

It would be easy to read that as a buy-in problem. It was not. They were running several searches on top of their actual jobs, and nothing reminded them to leave feedback, so they did not leave feedback.

What moved the needle was taking work off their plate: automations for candidates and for them, so scheduling and the back and forth about a candidate stopped depending on someone remembering.

If people are not following your process, assume the process is too expensive for them before you assume they do not care.

## Align on what the team needs, not what it would like to have

This is where I saw the most time burn. A hiring manager is sure a candidate is a 10 and a must-have, the candidate reaches the final interview, and a C-level does not see that need on that team. The candidate gets rejected, and weeks are gone.

That is not a bad interview. That is a kickoff that was never specific enough about what the role is for.

## You cannot find a bottleneck you are not tracking

We kept a record of every hire: how long from kickoff to offer, and from offer to start date, plus headcount and turnover by area. Simple tracking, not a dashboard project.

Time to fill came down over that year. What the tracking really gave us was the ability to point at the exact stage where things slowed down, instead of arguing about it.

## One more thing

We never piloted any of this. We rolled changes out and fixed them as we went, which is messier than it sounds in a case study but is the only way it happens at that speed.
