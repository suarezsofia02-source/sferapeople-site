Hiring senior engineers in Latin America can give a US startup strong talent and good time zone overlap. It also adds decisions you do not face when you hire next door: pay, setup, process and communication. Here is how we would approach it, step by step.

## 1. Define the role before you define the market

Write down what this person will own in the first six months. Be specific about the stack, the level of autonomy and the hours of overlap you need with your team. LatAm is not one market, and a search for "a senior engineer in LatAm" is much harder to run than a search for "a senior Node.js engineer in Argentina or Colombia with four hours of overlap."

## 2. Choose the setup early

Candidates and companies often discover too late that they want different things. In a poll of 203 people in our network, 59% preferred to be contractors paid in USD, 12% preferred local employment with USD pay, and 8% chose an EOR or PEO. 19% said it depends on the offer.

That is a preference, not a legal answer. Decide the setup with your lawyer or accountant before you start interviewing, and tell candidates in the first call. We go deeper in [Contractor, local employee or EOR](/insights/contractor-vs-employee-eor-latam-engineers).

## 3. Know what pay looks like, and share your range

Pay for senior engineers in LatAm varies by country, stack and contract type. In a second poll of 524 people in our network, 52% chose $5K to $7K per month, and 27% chose $8K or more. That poll has limits, and we explain them in [What senior engineers in LatAm earn per month](/insights/latam-senior-engineer-salary-poll).

In a third poll of 127 people, 58% said salary in USD is what matters most in an offer from a US startup, and 22% said growth and ownership. The full picture is in [What LatAm engineers want from a US job](/insights/what-latam-engineers-want-from-us-jobs). Use polls like this as one input. Compare with other salary reports and with the offers you have made before. Then share your range early, in the first message or call. It filters candidates quickly and shows respect for their time.

## 4. Run a short, structured process

Senior engineers have options, so a slow or unclear process costs you candidates. In a small poll of 67 people in our network, we asked what makes them drop out of a hiring process: 43% said 5+ interview rounds, 23% long take-home tests, 20% no salary range shared and 11% slow feedback (LinkedIn rounding makes the total 97%). With that few votes it is a signal, not a study, but it matches what we hear from candidates. A few habits that help:

- **Use a scorecard.** Decide the three to five things that matter for the role and score every candidate on the same ones, instead of relying on gut feeling.
- **Keep the number of rounds low.** Each extra round needs a reason.
- **Respect take-home tests.** Keep them short, relevant and paid if they are long.
- **Give feedback quickly.** Even a clear "no" is better than silence.

## 5. Make an offer that is clear on the details

A good offer states the pay, currency and payment schedule, the setup, the start date, the time zone expectations and any equipment or time off. If the setup is a contract, say so plainly. Confirm the legal terms with your lawyer before you send it.

## 6. Plan the first 30 days

The best hires still fail without a clear start. Share the first-month goals, who the engineer reports to, who will answer their questions, and how and when you will check in. A short written onboarding plan is enough.
